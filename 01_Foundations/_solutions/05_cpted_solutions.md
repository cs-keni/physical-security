## E5.1 — Full 7-step analysis, eight findings

### Marking criteria

Run the method as written: approach as each user type, map sightlines, inventory concealment,
survey lighting at night, read territoriality at each transition, walk maintenance, then write.
The failure mode is doing steps 1–6 in your head and jumping to step 7.

| Criterion | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| **Done at the right time of day** | The lighting survey is worthless in daylight, and the sightline map is different at 0600 in December. If every finding could have been written at 2 p.m. on a Tuesday, you skipped step 4. |
| **Every finding names the affected user** | "Second-shift staff departing after 2300," not "people." Findings that name a real population get funded; findings about "users" do not. |
| **Every finding names a mechanism** | *Why* does this condition enable an undesired act? Concealment within X feet of a path. No sightline from any occupied position. Ambiguous ownership at a transition. |
| **Every finding is costed and sequenced** | Immediate / short-term / capital, with a number. A finding without a cost is an opinion. |
| **At least two findings are free** | Trimming, relamping, moving furniture, changing a lighting schedule, unlocking or locking a door. If none of your eight is free, you are looking for products. |
| **At least one finding is a maintenance finding** | Step 6 exists because propped doors and graffiti are the highest-information observations on any site. |
| **At least one `[VERIFY]`** | Illuminance targets, uniformity ratios, and any local requirement. You do not know these from memory and should not pretend to. |

### Two worked findings, in the required format

> **CPTED-03 — Concealment at the north personnel entrance.**
> The north personnel door (used by approximately 40 staff arriving between 0530 and 0700, and
> the only entrance open before 0700) is set into a 4-foot-deep masonry recess. The recess is
> not visible from the guard post, from reception, or from any occupied interior position, and
> it is not covered by the existing camera, which is mounted above the door looking outward and
> therefore sees the parking lot rather than the recess itself. A person standing in the recess
> is unobserved from every direction until an arriving employee is within roughly 6 feet.
> **Recommend:** (1) relocate the existing camera to the light pole 30 ft north, facing the door,
> so that the recess and the approach are both in view [short-term, ~$1,800]; (2) add a
> downlight inside the recess head, which both removes the shadow and makes an occupant
> conspicuous rather than concealed [short-term, ~$900]; (3) at the next facade project,
> infill the recess or fit a glazed vestibule [capital, ~$25,000 — carry as a future item, do
> not recommend now].
> `[VERIFY]` Confirm resulting illuminance and uniformity at the door plane against IES
> guidance and any local requirement.

> **CPTED-06 — Propped door, west service corridor.**
> The west service door (Zone 1 → Zone 3, card reader, closer, no position switch) was observed
> propped with a fire extinguisher on three of three site visits, between 1000 and 1400. Kitchen
> staff use it to move deliveries from the loading apron to the kitchen, a route that otherwise
> requires a 140-foot detour through the public lobby with a food cart.
> **The propping is not a discipline problem; it is a design problem.** The access concept is
> fighting the shortest legitimate path, and it will keep losing.
> **Recommend:** (1) issue kitchen staff credentials with access to this door and add a
> position switch with a held-open alarm at 60 seconds [immediate, ~$400 plus credential
> admin]; (2) fit a hold-open device with an automatic release on the fire alarm so the door
> can be legitimately open during delivery windows [short-term, ~$1,400] `[CODE][VERIFY]` —
> confirm this is permitted for this opening; (3) **do not** simply remove the extinguisher and
> issue a memo. It will be propped again within a week and you will have spent your credibility
> instead of the client's money.

## E5.2 — Six conditions: strategy violated, low-cost and high-cost remedies

| | Condition | Strategy violated | Low-cost remedy | High-cost remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) | Garage stair tower, solid concrete, no windows | **Natural surveillance** | Improve interior lighting and paint walls and ceiling white to raise interior brightness and reflectance; add a convex mirror at each half-landing; add an intercom with a camera at each landing [~$3–6k] | Replace solid walls with glazing or open mesh so the stair is visible from the garage and from outside; this is how stairs should be built and it is a structural change [$100k+] |
| (b) | Retail storefront covered in promotional posters | **Natural surveillance** | Remove or relocate posters; enforce a "clear glazing between 3 ft and 6 ft" rule; move promotional material above 6 ft or below 3 ft [~$0] | Redesign the merchandising and signage program with the marketing function, including interior fixture heights, so the sightline is preserved by the standard rather than fought over annually [$15k+ of design time] |
| (c) | Four unlocked entrances, all equally prominent | **Natural access control** — no hierarchy, no primary entrance, no cue about where a visitor should go | Lock three; sign them as "exit only" or "employee entrance"; add directional signage at the property line and at each secondary door pointing to the main entrance [~$1–2k] | Architectural rework: canopy, lighting, paving, and landscaping to make one entrance unmistakably primary, with the others detailed as clearly secondary [$50k+] |
| (d) | Courtyard with dense 8-ft hedges along the main path | **Natural surveillance**, via the 3-foot/6-foot rule — shrubs above 3 ft and tree canopies below 6 ft both destroy sightlines and create concealment | Trim to 3 ft and add to the grounds maintenance contract with the height written into it [~$800 plus recurring] | Replace with a species that matures below 3 ft, or with a low wall or planted bed that defines the path without concealing anyone [$20k+] |
| (e) | Loading dock with a dumpster enclosure adjacent to the personnel door | **Natural surveillance** and **activity support** — a concealment position within feet of a controlled opening, in a zone with no legitimate observers | Relocate the dumpster enclosure away from the door and into a sightline; add lighting; add a camera with assessment coverage of the door approach [~$4–8k] | Rebuild the service yard so refuse, deliveries, and personnel access are separated, with the personnel door on a supervised elevation [$60k+] |
| (f) | Plaza with no seating, no shade, no reason to be there | **Activity support** (and **territorial reinforcement** — an unused space reads as unowned) | Seating, shade, a coffee cart or food truck licence, a lunchtime programme, bike racks — put legitimate people in the space [~$10–25k, and much of it is a leasing decision rather than a construction one] | Redesign the plaza with programmed uses, planting, and edge activation [$250k+] |

**Two things to notice across the table.**

First, **five of the six low-cost remedies are landscaping, lighting, signage, or furniture.**
That is the entire retrofit CPTED toolkit, and it is why the discipline is worth knowing even
when there is no construction budget.

Second, **(f) is the one people get wrong.** An empty plaza feels like a maintenance or design
problem, and the instinct is to add cameras. The mechanism is that an unused space has no
informal guardianship and signals that nobody is watching or claiming it — so the fix is
**people**, not surveillance. Adding cameras to an empty plaza gives you recorded evidence of an
event that a coffee cart would have prevented.

## E5.3 — The two-storey glass lobby with reception 60 feet back

### The CPTED problems

1. **No control point on the primary path.** Reception faces the elevator bank, which means it
   faces *away from the entrance*. Anyone entering has 60 feet of unobserved travel and can
   reach the elevators without ever entering the receptionist's field of view. This is not a
   surveillance failure — it is an **orientation** failure.
2. **No territorial transition.** A two-storey glass lobby entered at grade reads as public
   space. Nothing tells a stranger they have crossed a boundary, which is exactly the
   "anyone can walk from the public lobby to the executive floor" problem from lesson 02.
3. **The receptionist is isolated and exposed.** 60 feet from the door, with glass behind them
   at both levels, in a position where an incident at the desk is visible from the street but
   not reachable by anyone inside quickly. This is a **staff safety** finding, and it is the one
   most likely to change the architect's mind.
4. **The glazing cuts both ways.** Excellent natural surveillance *into* the lobby from outside
   during the day. At night, with the lobby lit and the exterior dark, it is a one-way mirror:
   the lobby is a lit stage and the exterior is invisible from within.
5. **Wayfinding is ambiguous**, which produces the failure mode CPTED cares about — a person
   with no legitimate business looks exactly like a person who is lost, and staff have no basis
   for challenging either.

### The solution that preserves the design intent

**Do not ask for the glass or the volume back.** The architect's intent is a generous,
light-filled, welcoming arrival, and every one of these problems can be solved without touching
that.

1. **Rotate and reposition the desk** so it faces the entry doors with the elevator bank in
   peripheral view, and move it forward to roughly 25–30 feet — still generous, still inside the
   volume, now on the path. **This is a furniture plan change and it costs nothing at this stage
   of design.** It is the single highest-value item on the list.
2. **Put the desk on the path rather than beside it.** Use floor material, a level change, a
   feature wall, or a planted line to make the natural walking route pass the desk on its way to
   the elevators. Architects generally like this, because it is a composition device rather than
   a barrier.
3. **Add a subtle territorial threshold** at roughly 15 feet in: a change in floor finish, a
   ceiling element, a slight narrowing. Enough that a stranger registers a transition, not
   enough to read as a checkpoint.
4. **Control the elevators, not the lobby.** Destination dispatch with credential-restricted
   floors means the *lobby* stays completely open and the *boundary* moves to the elevator call.
   This preserves the architect's public room entirely and puts the control where it is
   invisible.
5. **Solve the night condition with lighting design**, not with film or blinds: light the
   exterior approach so it is not a black mirror, and put the lobby on a reduced night scene.
6. **Give reception a duress device, a clear sightline to the security office or an intercom,
   and a desk detailed with a solid front and an unobstructed retreat path behind.** Furniture
   detail, not architecture.

### How to say it

> "The volume works and I don't want to change it. Three things would make it work
> operationally: turning the desk to face the doors and bringing it forward about thirty feet,
> putting a floor-material change on the walking line so people naturally pass it, and moving
> the access control to the elevator call instead of the lobby. That last one means the lobby
> stays completely open, which I think is what you want. Can we look at the furniture plan
> together?"

**"No" is not a deliverable.** The engineer who says the lobby is a security problem gets
overruled by the client, who wants the lobby. The engineer who says the lobby is fine and the
desk is in the wrong place gets the desk moved — and the desk was the actual finding.

## E5.4 — Allocate $15,000 at the shopping centre

### The conditions

Dark rear service alley · overgrown landscaping at the front entrances · graffiti on the east
wall · three burned-out lights · no signage at the property line · vacant unit with papered
windows.

### The allocation

| Order | Item | Cost | Why here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Relamp the three fixtures** | **~$1,200** | Cheapest possible restoration of a control that was already paid for and is currently doing nothing. Nothing else on this list has a better ratio. Do it this week. |
| **2** | **Remove the graffiti** | **~$1,500** | The maintenance / image mechanism is real and it is *time-dependent*. Graffiti that stays signals that nobody is watching and nobody owns the space, and it attracts more. Remove it now and establish a **48-hour removal standard** going forward — the standard is worth more than this one removal. |
| **3** | **Trim the front-entrance landscaping to the 3 ft / 6 ft rule** | **~$1,800** | Restores natural surveillance at the highest-traffic, highest-value locations. Include the first year of maintenance in the price and write the heights into the grounds contract, or it grows back and you buy it again. |
| **4** | **Light the rear service alley** | **~$6,500** | The most serious *condition* on the list and the most expensive fix, which is why it is fourth rather than first — items 1–3 together cost less than this one and address more ground. Two or three wall-pack or pole fixtures, specified for uniformity rather than raw output. `[VERIFY]` illuminance and uniformity against IES guidance and any local requirement. |
| **5** | **Property-line and wayfinding signage** | **~$2,500** | Territorial reinforcement: states ownership, states that the property is managed, and gives staff and police a basis for challenging presence. Cheap, permanent, and it does work no camera does. |
| **6** | **Address the vacant unit** | **~$1,500** | Replace papered windows with a professionally printed "coming soon" graphic or a vinyl display, and keep the interior lit on a timer. **Papered windows are the single strongest "this place is failing" signal on the site**, and they also create a blind frontage on a public elevation. |
| | **Total** | **~$15,000** | |

### Justifying the order

The ordering rule is **restore before you add, and buy signals before you buy hardware.**

- **Items 1, 2, and 3 restore things that already exist** — a lighting system that was designed
  and paid for, a wall that used to be clean, planting that was specified at the right height.
  Restoration is always cheaper than addition and it is what the maintenance/image strategy
  is about. Together they are 30% of the budget.
- **Item 4 is the only genuine capital addition**, and it is the one with the most severe
  condition behind it. It goes after the restorations not because the alley matters less, but
  because doing 1–3 first means that if the budget is cut mid-programme you have still bought
  the highest-ratio items.
- **Items 5 and 6 are signal purchases.** They change how the site reads to a person deciding
  whether this is a place where they will be noticed. That is the actual mechanism CPTED
  operates through, and it is nearly free compared with anything that draws power.
- **Nothing on this list is a camera**, and that is deliberate. $15,000 buys a small number of
  cameras and a recorder, with no operator, and it would have addressed none of the six
  conditions. If the client asks why there are no cameras, the answer is that cameras document
  and these measures prevent — and at this budget you can have one or the other.

**The recurring commitment matters more than the allocation.** Write into the report that the
landscaping heights and the 48-hour graffiti standard must go into the grounds and janitorial
contracts. Without that, three of these six items revert within a year and the money is spent
again.

## The thread through all four

E5.2 and E5.4 are about **restoring conditions**, not buying equipment. E5.3 is about **keeping
the room** — the finding was right and the way of saying it determines whether anything happens.
E5.1 is about **writing it down in a form that gets funded.**

CPTED's practical value to an engineer is that it is the only part of this discipline where the
best answer routinely costs under a thousand dollars. It is also the part most likely to be
dismissed, which is why the lesson's finding format — location, affected users, mechanism,
costed recommendation — matters more here than the strategies do.
