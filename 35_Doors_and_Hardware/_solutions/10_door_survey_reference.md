# Reference — The 10-Door Survey

> **Do the survey first.** Reading this before you go out gives you my findings instead of
> yours, and yours are the point.

This is a worked reference against a **fictional building** — Northgate Commons, a 1994
four-story suburban office building, 78,000 sq ft, multi-tenant, sprinklered, with a partial
2019 tenant fit-out on floors 2 and 3. Everything here is invented. No real facility, tenant, or
person is described. `[SYNTHETIC — per AI_CONTEXT.md hard rule 4]`

Read it for two things: **what a complete record looks like**, and **how findings get from
observations to a ranked memo**.

## Part 3 — The findings memo

> **Northgate Commons — Door and Hardware Survey**
> Ten openings surveyed 2026-08-07. Prepared by `[name]`.

### 1. Scope

Ten openings surveyed across levels 1–3 and the penthouse, selected to cover exterior glazed,
exterior service, stair, mechanical/electrical, telecom closet, tenant entry, magnetically
locked, paired, access-controlled, and unsecured categories. **Not covered:** level 4, the
parking structure, any opening inside a leased tenant suite, and the roof. No drawings, life
safety plan, keying schedule, or as-builts were available at the time of survey.

### 2. Method

Handing determined in the field per the standard convention, with the side stood on recorded for
every opening. Leaf dimensions measured; frame throat measured where accessible. Frame type
determined by visual inspection of the head/jamb joint; recorded as UNKNOWN where the joint was
concealed. Latching tested by releasing the door from approximately 5 degrees open at every
rated opening. Ceiling continuity checked at one location by lifting a corridor tile. No hardware
was disassembled and no concealed space was entered.

### 3. The three most significant findings

**Finding 1 — Opening 07: request-to-exit sensor mounted on the unsecured side.**

*Observed:* the REX motion sensor at the loading vestibule mag-locked opening is mounted on the
exterior (unsecured) side of the door, covering the approach from outside.

*Why it matters:* the REX shunts the door alarm on detection. Mounted on the unsecured side, it
shunts for anyone approaching from outside — so the door can be opened from the exterior with no
credential and the system records nothing abnormal. **The opening is functionally uncontrolled
and the audit log says it is fine.** No release button or signage was found either, which is a
separate and more serious problem at a magnetically locked opening in the egress path.

*Recommendation:* relocate the sensor to the secure side, covering the interior approach, and
verify coverage from oblique angles and at slow speeds. Separately and urgently: establish
whether this opening is on the means of egress and whether the required manual release and
signage are present. If they are absent, that is a life-safety item, not a security item.
`[CODE][VERIFY]`

*Effort:* sensor relocation is a half-day. The release/signage question needs the life safety
plan before it can be answered.

**Finding 2 — Opening 09: card reader through-bolted to a 90-minute rated frame.**

*Observed:* the penthouse mechanical room reader is mounted through the HM frame face. The leaf
carries a legible 90 MIN label.

*Why it matters:* an attachment beyond the listed preparation voids the assembly's label. The
opening currently carries a label stating a rating the assembly may no longer hold. This is the
worst class of defect because nothing downstream catches it — the label is present, the door
looks correct, and it will pass a casual look indefinitely. `[CODE][VERIFY]`

*Recommendation:* relocate the reader to the adjacent wall — nothing about its function requires
the frame. Then establish whether the frame penetration can be evaluated and the assembly
re-labeled by a party authorized by the listing agency; if not, the frame needs replacement.
Adopt "readers on the wall, never on a rated frame" as a standing rule for this building.

*Effort:* relocation is trivial. The remediation of the existing penetration is the unknown and
needs the listing agency's answer before it can be costed.

**Finding 3 — Opening 05 (and, by inference, every telecom and electrical closet in the 2019
fit-out): partitions terminate at the ceiling grid.**

*Observed:* the IDF-3 partition stops at the suspended ceiling. Confirmed by sighting along the
wall line from a corridor tile two bays away. The closet has a KD frame, no closer, and no
electronic access control. It was standing open at the time of survey.

*Why it matters:* the opening's delay value is bounded by the ceiling bypass, not the door.
Anyone can enter over the partition from the corridor in about a minute, with no tools and no
noise. Adding a better lock, or adding access control, would improve nothing — it would secure
the strongest path while the weakest stays open. This is the balanced-protection failure at the
scale of a single room.

*Recommendation, in order:*
1. Extend the partition to the structural deck, or install a hardened barrier above the ceiling
   line at the closet. This is architectural work and it is the only thing that changes the
   answer.
2. Only then consider access control at the door.
3. In the interim, and cheaply: a door position switch and an interior motion sensor on the
   intrusion system, so that entry by *either* path is detected. Detection is the affordable
   substitute when delay isn't available — see `../../01_Foundations/03_functional_chain.md`.
4. Fit a closer, so the door is at least closed.

*Effort:* item 3 is inexpensive and could be done this quarter. Item 1 is a capital item and
should be scoped across all closets in the 2019 fit-out, not just this one.

**Honourable mention — Opening 08:** the conference-level pair has an overlapping astragal and no
coordinator, so the leaves close out of order and the active leaf frequently does not latch. Low
security consequence at that location, but it is a $200 part fixing a door that does not close
properly, and if that opening is ever rated it becomes a compliance item.

### 4. Reported under rule 3

Nothing reported. No chained, blocked, or obstructed egress was found. **Opening 07's missing
release button and signage were escalated by email to `[facilities contact]` on 2026-08-07 as a
question rather than a finding**, pending confirmation of whether that opening is on the means
of egress — the survey could not establish that without the life safety plan.

## What to compare against your own memo

Three things distinguish a memo that gets acted on:

1. **The findings are ranked, and the ranking is defensible.** Finding 1 is first because it
   silently defeats a control. Finding 2 is second because it is a life-safety condition that
   nothing downstream will catch. Finding 3 is third because it is real and important but slower
   and more expensive to fix. A memo that lists observations in the order they were surveyed has
   not done the engineering.

2. **Each finding names the consequence, not just the condition.** "REX on the wrong side" is an
   observation. "The door opens from outside with no credential and the log says it's fine" is a
   finding.

3. **Section 5 exists.** A survey that reports only what was determined looks more complete and
   is less useful. Naming what you couldn't determine — and who owns each answer — is what turns
   a survey into the first step of a project rather than a standalone document.

**The most common failure in a first survey** is section 3 finding 3: identifying the IDF closet
as a lock problem. If you wrote "add a card reader to IDF-3," reread lesson 01 and then reread
`../../01_Foundations/04_defense_in_depth_and_zones.md`. The lock is the third-best attack path
at that opening, and buying delay at the strongest point is the most expensive way to stay
insecure.
